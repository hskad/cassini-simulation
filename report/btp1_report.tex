\begin{abstract}
Communication alignment can reduce network contention, but applying a shift
also adds waiting time. We study shift costs and traffic-score accuracy in a
lightweight Cassini-based simulator. A remaining-work benefit rule accepts a
change only when it predicts a reduction in mean remaining completion time
after accounting for the added wait. On a 200-job synthetic short-job workload,
the rule reduces mean
completion time by 9.30 percent relative to the compatibility-based baseline
with identical placement, averaged over three seeds. Systematic remaining-work
estimate errors of up to 50 percent retain gains between 8.07 and 10.35 percent
on this workload. A separate phase-event check evaluates traffic scores over
complete common periods. The 600 millisecond score is optimistic in 15 of 21
synthetic cases, although the loss in selected-shift quality is much smaller.
The results identify why waiting cost and score accuracy must be checked
separately. Completion-time gains follow the simulator's runtime model; they
are not measurements from deployed training systems.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Distributed training jobs exchange gradients and activations over shared
network links. Their communication phases can overlap and exceed a link's
capacity. Cassini considers periodic communication patterns during placement
and shifts training iterations to reduce such overlap. Its geometric
representation and affinity graph coordinate jobs across shared
links~\cite{cassini}.

A shift adds waiting time that must be recovered through less contention
during the remaining work. A long job may recover this cost, while a short job
may finish sooner if it keeps its current offset. Repeated changes can add
further waiting. A decision rule should therefore consider the expected
completion-time benefit as well as the traffic compatibility score.

A second issue concerns the interval used to evaluate compatibility. For
jobs with different iteration periods, the full combined pattern repeats
after their least common multiple (LCM). This interval can become large even
when each job has a short period. The simulator limits its default scoring
window to 600~ms to bound the calculation cost. A shift that looks favourable
within that window may encounter greater traffic overlap later. Score
accuracy and shift quality are distinct: several shifts may have similar
long-term quality despite different short-window scores.

We investigate these questions using a Cassini-based simulator that advances
between job arrivals and completions. It estimates contention from periodic
bandwidth profiles and charges the waiting time caused by offset changes.
Every policy receives identical jobs and fixed job-to-link placement, so
placement differences are excluded from the shift-policy comparison.

The first study compares a remaining-work benefit rule with the starting
compatibility-based model, a score control, and a cooldown control. The benefit
rule compares estimated mean remaining completion times with and without a
proposed shift. We also perturb its remaining-work estimates while keeping
actual job lengths unchanged.

The second study integrates offered bandwidth between phase-boundary events
and checks selected shifts over a later complete LCM interval. Short-period
controls and longer-period cases reveal the effect of truncation. The study
measures score error, shift quality, selection time, and traced memory
allocation independently of the simulator's completion-time estimates.

The completed work has three main contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible fast simulator with explicit shift-wait accounting
    and controlled comparisons under identical placement.
    \item An evaluation of remaining-work shift decisions against score and
    cooldown controls, including tests with systematic estimate errors.
    \item An independent LCM scoring study that separates optimistic scores
    from losses in selected-shift quality and measures their calculation cost.
\end{itemize}

The workloads are synthetic or illustrative, rather than verified production
records. Completion-time results follow a compatibility-based slowdown model;
individual training iterations and network transfers are not executed. The
separate traffic checker validates offered-load calculations, but does not
validate the mapping from compatibility to completion time. These limits
define the scope of our comparison with Cassini's published system.
