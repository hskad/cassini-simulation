\begin{abstract}
Training jobs can slow each other down when they send data over the same
network link at the same time. Cassini reduces this overlap by choosing where
jobs run and adjusting when they communicate. We build a discrete-event simulator
based on this approach and study when its timing adjustments are worthwhile.
Our shift-selection rule uses the work left in each job to check whether a
new delay is expected to shorten average completion time. On a 200-job
synthetic short-job workload, it reduces mean completion time by 9.30 percent
compared with selecting shifts from traffic overlap alone, averaged over
three seeds with the same jobs and placement. Further experiments assess
sensitivity to errors in work estimates. A separate traffic study compares
scores from short evaluation intervals with scores over a complete repeating
pattern. Together, these simulation studies use synthetic and illustrative
workloads to examine the trade-offs between shift cost, completion time,
and traffic-score accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Training a model on several graphics processing units (GPUs) requires both
computation and communication. Workers calculate model updates and exchange
data so that training can continue. In a shared cluster, several jobs may
use the same network links. If their communication bursts occur together,
their combined demand can exceed the available bandwidth. Training can then
slow down even when sufficient compute resources have been assigned.

The timing of these bursts offers a scheduling opportunity. While one job
computes, another may be able to use the network. Coordinating them could
reduce competition for a link without increasing its capacity or changing
the model being trained. We chose communication scheduling as our problem
because it offers this scheduling-level way to improve resource sharing.

Cassini provides a concrete starting point for studying this idea. It extends
an existing job scheduler with information about repeating communication
patterns. It compares possible worker placements and calculates delays that
help jobs share network links at different times~\cite{cassini}. Implementing
these ideas gives us a testbed in which the waiting cost and traffic score
of each decision can be examined separately.

Our investigation focuses on the difference between a favourable traffic
pattern and a useful scheduling decision. Creating a new alignment requires
a job to wait. A long job may recover this cost through less competition
later; a short job may finish sooner if it keeps its current timing. The
decision also depends on the score used to predict traffic overlap. Examining
only the beginning of a repeating pattern saves calculation time, but can
miss overlap that occurs later.

These observations lead to two research questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item When does a timing shift save enough time to justify the wait it adds?
    \item How does a shorter traffic-evaluation interval affect score accuracy
    and the quality of the selected shifts?
\end{enumerate}

We address the first question with a rule that considers the work remaining
in each affected job before accepting a new shift. We compare it with the
starting simulator and two simpler controls. The score control accepts only
higher scores; the cooldown control limits how often shifts can change.
We also test errors in the remaining-work estimates. Every policy receives
identical jobs and fixed job-to-link placement. We address the second question with a separate
traffic checker that evaluates selected shifts over a complete repeating
pattern. This separates score validation from the simulator's completion-time
formula.

The completed work has three main contributions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A reproducible fast simulator with explicit shift-wait accounting
    and controlled comparisons under identical placement.
    \item An evaluation of remaining-work shift decisions against score and
    cooldown controls, including tests with systematic estimate errors.
    \item An independent LCM scoring study that separates optimistic scores
    from losses in selected-shift quality and measures their calculation cost.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background: How Cassini Coordinates Jobs}
\label{sec:cassini-background}

\subsection{Computation, Communication, and Timing Shifts}

A training iteration can contain intervals of computation and intervals of
network communication. A \emph{traffic profile} describes the bandwidth a job
requests over time. When the iteration repeats, a similar profile may repeat
with it. Different jobs can have different iteration lengths and several
communication bursts within one iteration.

Consider a simplified example with two jobs, each computing for 60~ms and
communicating for 40~ms per iteration. Assume each job needs the full link
capacity while communicating. If they start together, both request
the network during the same final 40~ms. Delaying the second job by 40~ms
separates their communication intervals in this ideal repeating pattern.
This delay is a \emph{time shift}. The example explains the opportunity, but
also its cost: the second job must first wait for 40~ms.

\subsection{Placement and Compatibility}

\emph{Placement} determines which servers run a job's workers and therefore
which network links carry its traffic. Cassini evaluates candidate placements
provided by an underlying scheduler. It favours combinations whose traffic
patterns can be interleaved on shared links. Its compatibility score expresses
how well those patterns can fit together after suitable shifts~\cite{cassini}.

To calculate shifts, Cassini represents a repeating iteration as a circle.
Intervals on the circle describe its traffic demand; rotating the circle
changes the job's timing offset. For jobs with different periods, the common
representation uses their \emph{least common multiple} (LCM). For example,
40~ms and 60~ms periods repeat together every 120~ms. Larger combinations can
produce a much longer common period and a more expensive calculation.

\subsection{Coordinating Jobs Across Links}

A job can use several links and share each with different jobs. Separate
link-level decisions might therefore request different shifts for the same
job. Cassini represents these relationships with an \emph{affinity graph}.
Its two types of vertices represent jobs and links; an edge indicates that
a job uses a link. For an acyclic graph, its traversal method obtains one
consistent shift for each job. The published method rejects placement
candidates whose affinity graphs contain cycles. Server agents then apply
the selected delays to the next iteration and correct timing drift during
execution~\cite{cassini}.

\subsection{Connection to Our Study}

The published system already applies real delays to training iterations.
Our first study evaluates a completion-time test for selecting shifts in
a Cassini-based discrete-event model. The second study compares a 600~ms
evaluation interval, chosen for our implementation, with longer intervals
and a complete common period. These studies connect the timing cost of a
scheduling decision with the traffic information used to make it.

The evaluation uses synthetic and illustrative workloads. The fast simulator
advances between job arrivals and completions, accounts for shift waiting
time, and estimates job slowdown from compatibility. This event-level model
supports controlled comparisons of scheduling policies. A separate traffic
checker calculates requested bandwidth over time to assess score accuracy
and selected-shift quality. Completion-time results therefore describe the
simulator's runtime model, while traffic results describe the evaluated
bandwidth profiles.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Communication-Aware Training Schedules}

Cassini combines worker placement with communication timing. Its geometric
representation and affinity graph let the scheduler compare how jobs share
links~\cite{cassini}. Our implementation uses these ideas as the basis for
controlled experiments. We hold job-to-link placement fixed across policies
and study the decision to accept a proposed shift. This isolates the effect
of timing decisions on modelled completion time. Our score and cooldown
controls are experimental decision rules introduced in this study.

\subsection{Scheduling with a Cost of Change}

Changing a schedule can consume time before it produces a benefit. Eclipse
studies this trade-off in hybrid circuit and packet networks, where circuit
reconfiguration introduces a delay. Its scheduling method balances this
cost against the traffic served by a configuration~\cite{eclipse}. This
provides a related design principle for our remaining-work rule: a shift
should offer enough expected benefit to cover its waiting cost. We apply
that principle to training-job offsets and estimated remaining completion
time, whereas Eclipse schedules switch configurations and traffic service.

\subsection{Offsets in Periodic Systems}

Offset assignment also appears in real-time systems. Ladeira \emph{et al.}
propose GCD+ for periodic tasks under a FIFO protocol and evaluate it using
synthetic task sets and a UAV telemetry application~\cite{gcdplus}. This work
shows the wider use of timing offsets to manage repeated resource demands.
Our traffic study examines bandwidth profiles on shared links and the
evaluation interval used to score them. It measures score accuracy and
selected-shift quality as the common period grows. Together, these studies
place our work at the intersection of communication scheduling, change-cost
decisions, and periodic traffic analysis.

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:system-model}

\subsection{Jobs, Profiles, and Shared Links}

A job $j$ arrives at time $a_j$ and contains $N_j$ training iterations.
Its nominal iteration period $T_j$ is the sum of its phase durations.
A periodic profile $b_j(u)$ gives its requested bandwidth at phase position
$u\in[0,T_j)$. Computation phases have zero bandwidth demand; communication
phases have a specified demand. Durations are expressed in milliseconds and
bandwidth in gigabits per second.

For the policy comparisons, each job is assigned to one primary bottleneck
link. A seeded placement map is generated once per workload case and shared
by all policies. Let $\mathcal{J}_{\ell}$ denote the active jobs assigned to
link $\ell$, whose capacity is $C_{\ell}$. A positive offset $\theta_j$
delays the profile. The aggregate requested bandwidth in the scoring model is
\begin{equation}
 B_{\ell}(u;\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
 \sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}_{\ell}}
 b_j\bigl((u-\theta_j)\bmod T_j\bigr).
 \label{eq:offered-load}
\end{equation}
The coordinate $u$ describes periodic profile alignment. Arrival times and
finite job lengths are handled separately by the event simulator.

\subsection{Traffic Compatibility and Shift Proposals}

For a scoring interval with $M$ samples at spacing $\delta$, compatibility
is defined by the normalised excess demand:
\begin{equation}
 S_{\ell} = 1 - \frac{1}{M C_{\ell}}
 \sum_{k=0}^{M-1}
 \max\{0,B_{\ell}(k\delta;\boldsymbol{\theta})-C_{\ell}\}.
 \label{eq:compatibility}
\end{equation}
A score of one indicates that sampled demand stays within capacity. Larger
excess demand lowers the score, which can be negative under heavy load.
Links with at most one active job receive a score of one in the runtime
model; the evaluated profiles have individual demands within link capacity.

At the default $\delta=1$~ms resolution, the scoring interval is the smaller
of the sampled common period and the configured limit $W=600$~ms. Both the
shift search and runtime scoring use this interval. The search anchors the
first job at zero and adds the other jobs greedily. It checks offsets at
1~ms spacing for two-job links and 2~ms spacing for larger groups, retaining
the candidate with the smallest sampled excess demand at each step.

The decision policy then accepts the proposed offsets or retains the
previous offsets. Retained offsets are scored again for the current set of
active jobs. This keeps each runtime score consistent with the timing plan
that the simulator actually applies.

\subsection{Shift Waiting and Event Progress}

Let $\theta_j$ be the applied offset and $\theta'_j$ a proposed offset,
both reduced modulo $T_j$. Applying a change adds the forward wait
\begin{equation}
 d_j = (\theta'_j-\theta_j)\bmod T_j.
 \label{eq:shift-wait}
\end{equation}
An unchanged offset adds no wait. A smaller target offset wraps forward
through the next period. Added waits accumulate with any pending wait, and
a job makes progress only after its pending wait has elapsed.

The simulator processes arrivals and predicted completions. In the
incremental mode, an event updates the affected link. In the global mode,
it recalculates the active cluster plan. Incremental mode uses the affected
link score; global mode uses the mean score over links with multiple jobs,
or one when there are no such links. The placement map remains fixed in
both modes for the policy experiments.

Given the applicable score $S$, the model uses the slowdown factor
\begin{equation}
 F(S)=1+\lambda(1-S),
 \label{eq:slowdown}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is a configured penalty, set to 1.5 in the policy
experiments. This factor scales the whole nominal iteration time. If
$r_j$ is the completed fraction of a job, its remaining nominal work is
$R_j=(1-r_j)N_jT_j$. At event time $t$, with pending wait $w_j$, its
predicted completion is
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{c}_j=t+w_j+R_jF(S).
 \label{eq:predicted-completion}
\end{equation}
Between events, useful elapsed time advances the completed fraction at the
current effective rate. New events update the score and completion
prediction while preserving completed work and pending waits.

\subsection{Completion Metrics and Independent Traffic Checks}

The recorded job completion time is $\mathrm{JCT}_j=c_j-a_j$, where $c_j$
is the simulated completion timestamp. We compare mean and 95th-percentile
JCT and record cumulative shift waits and the number of offset changes.
Paired policy comparisons use the same arrivals, job profiles, iteration
counts, and placement map.

The LCM study uses a separate phase-boundary checker to integrate excess
requested bandwidth. For integer-millisecond periods, let
$H=\operatorname{lcm}(T_1,\ldots,T_n)$. Its full-period score is
\begin{equation}
 S_H=1-\frac{1}{H C_{\ell}}\int_H^{2H}
 \max\{0,B_{\ell}(u;\boldsymbol{\theta})-C_{\ell}\}\,du.
 \label{eq:full-period-score}
\end{equation}
The checker integrates the constant-demand intervals between phase
boundaries directly. We compare a plan's short-window score with its
full-period score to measure score optimism. We also compare plans selected
under different window limits using this common full-period measure. The
runtime experiments thus assess completion time within the event model,
while the traffic experiments assess the repeating demand patterns directly.
